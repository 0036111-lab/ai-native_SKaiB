# How the Most Memorable Names Are Created

What separates a name you remember after one exposure from one that evaporates is
not luck — it is a small set of mechanisms studied in psycholinguistics and
consumer research. Read this before a generation wave so you generate *for*
memorability, and lean on it again when scoring **Memorability** in the rubric.

The headline finding: **memorability lives in the tension between two forces** —
*processing fluency* (easy to take in) and *distinctiveness* (different enough to
stand out). Names that are only fluent are forgettable; names that are only
distinctive are unpronounceable. The winners sit in the narrow band that is both.

---

## 1. The fluency × distinctiveness sweet spot

- **Processing fluency** — we like and remember what is easy to perceive, say, and
  encode. Easy-to-pronounce names are judged more favourably and recalled more
  readily than hard ones. This is why an unspellable coinage loses even when it is
  "unique".
- **Distinctiveness (the Von Restorff / isolation effect)** — in a field of similar
  items, the one that breaks the pattern is the one remembered. Distinctiveness is
  what fluency alone cannot buy.
- **The sweet spot** — *unusual enough to be distinctive, familiar enough to be
  fluent*. **Google** is the canonical example: a novel token (a play on *googol*)
  that nonetheless obeys ordinary English phonotactics, so it reads as instantly
  pronounceable *and* unmistakably new. Aim every candidate at this band; reject
  names that buy distinctiveness with a spelling/pronunciation tax.

## 2. Sound symbolism — the meaning carried by the sound itself

Phonemes carry connotation before the brain decodes meaning. Exploit it
deliberately:

- **Plosives / hard stops (k, p, t, b, d, g)** — especially as the *initial* sound,
  drive recall, recognition, and an impression of impact and energy (**K**odak,
  **K**leenex, **G**oogle, **N**i**k**e, **T**ik**T**ok). A clean terminal stop
  also helps the name "land".
- **Front vowels (the "ee" in *meet*, the "i" in *slim*)** connote *small, light,
  fast, sharp*. **Back vowels (the "o" in *go*, "u" in *blue*)** connote *large,
  heavy, round, slow*. Match the vowel to the brand's promise (a speed product
  leans front; a premium, substantial one leans back).
- **Fricatives (f, v, s, z)** suggest *smoothness, speed, flow* (**S**tripe,
  **V**isa, **Z**ara).
- **Sound-symbolic affixes** carry their own signal: `st-` → strength/stability
  (Stripe, Stack, Starbucks); `-ix`/`-ex` → technical precision (Xerox, Kleenex);
  `-ify`/`-ly` → action/transformation (Spotify, Shopify).

Names that **obey sound-symbolism rules test as more memorable** than names that
fight them — and a name whose sound *contradicts* its meaning (a soft, slow-sounding
name for a speed product) quietly underperforms.

## 3. Rhythm, stress, and length

- **Stress pattern.** A strong **trochaic** (STRONG-weak) rhythm is stickier:
  **GOO**-gle, **STAR**-bucks, **CO**-ca-**CO**-la. Reduplication and clear beats
  (Coca-Cola, TikTok, Kit-Kat) ride the same effect.
- **Syllables.** **1–3 syllables** is the memorable range; two is the comfortable
  default. Beyond three, recall drops.
- **Length in characters.** Names of roughly **6–8 characters** consistently win
  memory tests — long enough to be distinctive, short enough to encode in one pass.
  This is a guide for single tokens, not a hard cap on two-word names.

## 4. Distinctiveness through unexpected pairing (the "idea × idea" engine)

The most reliable way to manufacture distinctiveness *without* sacrificing fluency
is to **cross two familiar words into an unexpected pair**. Each word is fluent on
its own; their collision is what the brain stops on and stores.

- **Netflix** = internet × flicks. **Pinterest** = pin × interest. **Instagram** =
  instant × telegram.
- Two-word names do this overtly: **The North Face**, **Red Bull**, **Free
  People**, **Teenage Engineering**. The pairing produces **idea × idea**
  (multiplicative — a small story the mind completes) rather than **idea + idea**
  (additive — a description it forgets). Neither half should stand alone.

This is the cognitive justification for the **two-word cross** (technique 10 in
`construction-techniques.md`) and for not letting a wave collapse into only
one-word coinages: the cross is a distinctiveness *generator*, and it also clears
trademark/domain screening far more often than a lone invented token.

## 5. Concrete imagery and emotion

- **Concreteness beats abstraction.** Names that trigger a vivid mental picture
  (**Amazon**, **Apple**, **Caterpillar**, **Patagonia**) are encoded with a
  visual hook and recalled better than abstract or purely functional names. This is
  the "I" (Imagery) in SMILE.
- **Emotion deepens the trace.** A name that produces an affective response is
  stored with that feeling attached; fluent processing is itself read as a faint
  *positive* signal ("this feels familiar/trustworthy"), which is one route from
  easy-to-say to easy-to-like to easy-to-remember.

## 6. Memorability checklist (apply per candidate)

Score a candidate higher on **Memorability** when it:

- [ ] Sits in the **fluent × distinctive** band (new but instantly sayable).
- [ ] Opens on a **hard stop** or uses sound symbolism that *matches* its meaning.
- [ ] Has a clear **stress/rhythm** and lands in **1–3 syllables** / ~6–8 chars
      (for a single token).
- [ ] If multi-word, the two parts form an **idea × idea** cross, not a description.
- [ ] Triggers a **concrete image** and/or an **emotion**.
- [ ] Survives the **say-it-once test**: heard once, can a stranger spell it and
      repeat it? (Fails SCRATCH "Spelling challenge"/"Hard to pronounce" → near-veto.)
- [ ] Passes the **sound-alike screen**: said aloud (and slurred/fast), it does *not*
      resemble a negative or taboo word — memorability is worthless if the thing it
      makes memorable is the wrong word. Pronunciation drives word-of-mouth, the
      real engine of virality. See `sound-alike-screen.md`.

> Caveat: every rule here has a famous exception (Häagen-Dazs is deliberately
> unpronounceable; Xerox opens on a soft "z" sound). Exceptions work when backed by
> large marketing budgets — treat these as the defaults for resource-constrained
> naming, not laws.
