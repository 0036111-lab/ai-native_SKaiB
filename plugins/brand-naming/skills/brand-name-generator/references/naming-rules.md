# Naming Rules, Taxonomy & Failure Modes

Distilled defaults from professional naming practice. Every rule has exceptions
(Häagen-Dazs breaks most of them) — but exceptions require capital to overcome.
Treat these as defaults for resource-constrained situations.

## Taxonomy of distinctiveness (USPTO spectrum, weakest → strongest protection)

| Level | What it is | Examples | TM strength | Marketing cost | Global scaling | Short-term SEO |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | The common name of the thing itself | "Search Engine" | None | Very low | Poor | Highest |
| **Descriptive** | Directly describes a feature/quality | Hotels.com, PayPal, The Body Shop | Weak | Low | Limited | High |
| **Suggestive** | Implies a benefit; needs a small imaginative leap | Netflix, Stripe, Amazon, Pinterest | Strong | Medium | Good | Medium |
| **Arbitrary** | Real word, unrelated context | Apple, Oracle, Caterpillar, Virgin | Very strong | High | Excellent | Low |
| **Coined / Fanciful** | Invented word, no prior meaning | Kodak, Verizon, Xerox, Häagen-Dazs | Strongest | Highest | Excellent | Lowest |

**Choosing:** well-funded brand building for long-term equity → arbitrary or
coined. Bootstrapped, SEO-led, single-market → descriptive can be rational.
Most successful modern startups land in **suggestive**.

## SMILE & SCRATCH heuristic

**SMILE — what makes a name powerful:**
- **S**uggestive — evokes the brand's essence/benefit
- **M**eaningful — positive connotations in target markets
- **I**magery — triggers a strong visual/mental picture
- **L**egs — supports extension and storytelling over time
- **E**motional — creates authentic connection

**SCRATCH — what makes a name fail:**
- **S**pelling challenge — can't spell it after hearing it once
- **C**opycat — too close to a competitor
- **R**estrictive — limits geographic or product-line expansion
- **A**nnoying — irritating to say/hear repeatedly
- **T**ame — generic, unmemorable, interchangeable
- **C**urse of knowledge — only makes sense to insiders
- **H**ard to pronounce — globally or locally ambiguous

## Core technical rules

- **Phonetics.** Hard stop consonants (K, X, T, P, B) aid memorability (Kodak,
  Google, Nike). Vowel-dominant names feel softer/approachable. **1–2 syllables**
  is ideal for recall; 3 can work with clear rhythm (A-ma-zon).
- **Word count.** A name need not be one word. Most successful modern brands are
  **one *or* two words** — and the two-word "idea × idea" cross (The North Face,
  Red Bull, Free People, Teenage Engineering) is often more distinctive *and*
  easier to clear for trademark/domain than a lone coinage. Generate both; never
  let a wave or shortlist collapse to one-word names only. Three words is the
  ceiling (Palo Alto Networks). See `construction-techniques.md` (technique 10) and
  `name-memorability.md`.
- **Memorability mechanics.** Memorability is the tension between *processing
  fluency* (easy to say) and *distinctiveness* (breaks the pattern — the Von
  Restorff effect). Sit in the band that is both. Full detail, with sound
  symbolism, rhythm, and the say-it-once test, in `name-memorability.md`.
- **Sound symbolism.** `st-` openings suggest strength/stability (Stripe, Stack,
  Starbucks). `-ix`/`-ex` endings suggest technical precision (Xerox, Kleenex).
  `-ify`/`-ly` suggest action/transformation (Spotify, Shopify).
- **Visual readability.** Must read cleanly in Latin script, at tiny sizes
  (favicon, app icon), in ALL CAPS and all-lower. Avoid ambiguous pairs:
  `rn`↔`m`, `cl`↔`d`, `li`↔`u`, `vv`↔`w`.
- **Global pronounceability.** Sanity-check the sound in Mandarin, Spanish,
  Arabic, Japanese, German — the five languages covering the largest consumer
  populations. "Zara" works everywhere because z/a exist in all major systems.
- **Trademark strength.** Aim arbitrary or coined; avoid describing your category.
- **Negative-connotation check.** Neutral in English ≠ neutral elsewhere
  (Mitsubishi Pajero, Ford Pinto in Brazil). Must ultimately be checked by native
  speakers, not translation software — AI only pre-screens.
- **Pronunciation / sound-alike check.** Screen what the name *sounds like*, not
  only how it is spelled — word-of-mouth runs on sound, so a name that *reads* clean
  but *sounds* like a negative word (**Engree** ≈ angry, **Keel** ≈ kill) will hurt
  virality. Say it aloud (carefully, fast, and in the target accents), read the
  run-together slug, and check the five-language *sound*. A clear negative
  sound-alike is a near-veto. Full stop-word list and sound patterns in
  `sound-alike-screen.md`.
- **Uniqueness in context.** Stand out from direct competitors *and* adjacent
  categories. Launching "HomeBnB" against Airbnb starts disadvantaged.
- **Domain/handle reality.** A perfect name with a so-so domain is still usable
  (.co/.io/.ai are legitimate). A name blocked across *all* TLDs and *all* major
  handles is genuinely problematic.
- **Legal clearance is non-negotiable.** A name without a cleared trademark is a
  liability, not a name. (This skill does not provide it — flag it.)

## Key failure modes to avoid (process-level)

- **Generating without a brief.** Keyword-in, list-out is a toy.
- **Skipping trademark/availability screening.** Presenting unscreened names =
  presenting a liability list.
- **Treating AI linguistic screening as sufficient.** AI misses recent slang,
  regional variation, current events. Native review is required for international use.
- **Over-engineering generation.** 20 screened candidates beat 500 raw ones.
- **Presenting names ranked.** Position #1 gets anchored as "the recommendation".
  Present a portfolio of viable options instead.
